# Marketing Principles Reference

The library `/gtm critic` cites when a finding names the principle it breaks. Attribution is honest: public frameworks are credited to their authors; rules with no single canonical source are marked practitioner consensus rather than given an invented citation. Cite the principle by its name below, with the source in parentheses.

Format per entry: the rule, then what a violation looks like in the wild.

---

## 1. Rule of One (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers)

One reader, one big idea, one offer, one promise per piece. Copy that tries to persuade everyone at once persuades no one.

**Violation looks like:** a landing page addressing three audiences in one hero; two competing CTAs in the same view; a cold email making two asks.

## 2. 4U Headline Checklist (Michael Masterson, taught via AWAI)

A strong headline is Useful (names a benefit), Urgent (a reason to act now), Unique (says what nothing else in the category says), and Ultra-specific (concrete detail or numbers). Few headlines score on all four; scoring on none is a failure.

**Violation looks like:** "Welcome to [Product]" as an H1; a headline that is a category label ("AI-powered analytics") with no benefit, urgency, or specificity.

## 3. Positioning Components (April Dunford, Obviously Awesome)

Positioning holds only as a chain: competitive alternatives (what the customer would do without you) -> unique attributes (what you have that the alternatives lack) -> value with proof (what those attributes enable) -> best-fit customers (who cares most about that value) -> market category (the frame that makes the value obvious). A claim at any link that is not grounded in the link before it is assertion, not positioning.

**Violation looks like:** claiming a category with no unique attributes underneath it; naming a differentiator no listed competitor actually lacks; "best for everyone" as the customer definition.

## 4. Competitor-Swap Test (practitioner consensus)

If a direct rival's name fits your headline or value proposition unchanged, it is not positioning - it is a category description. Differentiation must be falsifiable: something a specific competitor could not honestly claim.

**Violation looks like:** "The fastest way to manage your projects" - true of every project tool's homepage; positioning statements built entirely from category-generic benefits.

## 5. Value Equation (Alex Hormozi, $100M Offers)

Perceived value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay x Effort & Sacrifice). The first two build value; the last two destroy it. An offer is weak when the outcome is vague, the likelihood is unproven, the time-to-result is hidden, or the required effort is understated.

**Violation looks like:** an offer promising a vague outcome ("grow your business") with no proof of likelihood; hiding a long setup behind "get started in minutes"; a guarantee that quietly excludes the main failure case.

## 6. Jobs-to-be-Done (Clayton Christensen)

Customers hire a product to make progress in a specific circumstance - functional, social, and emotional. Messaging that describes the product instead of the progress it enables misses the reason anyone buys.

**Violation looks like:** feature lists with no circumstance ("real-time sync, custom fields, API access"); personas defined by demographics instead of the struggle that triggers a purchase.

## 7. AIDA (lineage of E. St. Elmo Lewis)

Attention, Interest, Desire, Action - in that order. A piece that asks for action before building interest and desire converts only the already-convinced.

**Violation looks like:** a cold email whose first line is the demo ask; a page whose only content between headline and signup form is a feature grid.

## 8. Problem-Agitate-Solve (practitioner consensus)

Name the reader's problem in their words, make its cost concrete, then present the solution. Solution-first copy reads as self-praise; problem-first copy reads as understanding.

**Violation looks like:** body copy that never names the pain the ICP actually has; "we built X because we love Y" origin framing doing the work a problem statement should.

## 9. Social Proof and Authority (Robert Cialdini, Influence)

People resolve uncertainty by looking at what similar others do and what credible authorities endorse - and borrowed credibility must be real and checkable. Proof works hardest adjacent to the claim it supports.

**Violation looks like:** "trusted by thousands" with no names or numbers; logos of companies that are not actually customers; a strong claim with all testimonials three screens away.

## 10. Honest Scarcity and Loss Aversion (lineage of Kahneman & Tversky; Cialdini on scarcity)

Losses loom larger than gains, and scarcity moves people - which is exactly why fabricated urgency is poison: the first fake deadline a buyer catches costs all future trust.

**Violation looks like:** countdown timers that reset; "only 3 spots left" on a self-serve SaaS; "limited-time" offers with no stated end.

## 11. Benefit Over Feature - the "so what?" test (practitioner consensus)

Every feature claim must survive "so what?" - translated into an outcome the reader wants. Features are evidence; benefits are the argument.

**Violation looks like:** spec-sheet copy for a non-technical buyer; benefits so abstract ("save time", "work smarter") they no longer refer to anything.

## 12. Message Match (practitioner consensus, paid-traffic and CRO practice)

The page must repeat the promise of the thing that was clicked - same offer, same wording, same audience. Every gap between the source's promise and the landing headline is paid for in bounces.

**Violation looks like:** an ad promising a template pack landing on a generic homepage; a launch post promising "for indie hackers" landing on enterprise-toned copy.

## 13. 5-Second Clarity Test (practitioner consensus, usability testing practice)

A first-time visitor should know what it is, who it is for, and why it matters within five seconds of the hero. Clever that costs clarity is expensive.

**Violation looks like:** metaphor-only headlines ("Your second brain for everything"); pages where the product's category is only discoverable in the footer.

## 14. Stage Fit (Adaptico OS methodology)

Advice must match the founder's tier: pre-validation founders need positioning and conversations, not SEO campaigns; scaling founders need retention and durable channels, not another rebrand. Premature tactics burn runway and corrupt the read on product-market fit.

**Violation looks like:** a report recommending paid ads to a Tier 1 founder; a content-calendar plan for a founder with no audience and an unvalidated ICP.

## 15. Evidence Discipline (practitioner consensus)

Never present an invented or estimated number as a measured one. Every metric traces to a source - the page, the profile, a named benchmark - or it is labeled as an assumption or a gap. Reports that guess quietly train the reader to distrust the ones that measured.

**Violation looks like:** "your conversion rate is likely 2.1%" with no data; industry benchmarks cited without naming the range's source; a score delta claimed against a baseline that was never recorded.
